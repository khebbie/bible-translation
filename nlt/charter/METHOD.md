# NLT — Translation Charter (distilled)

Position on the spectrum: **dynamic / functional equivalence — meaning-based.** The
freest of the three.

Primary sources: `nlt2-intro.md` (2nd ed. introduction, verbatim — the authoritative
statement), `nlt1-intro.md` (1st ed., 1996), `tyndale-nlt-faq.md` (publisher FAQ).

## Core principle

> "The goal of this translation theory is to produce in English the **closest natural
> equivalent of the message** expressed by the original-language text, both in meaning
> and in style."

Note "and in style" — the NLT is not merely paraphrasing content; it aims to
reproduce *effect*. The working question is: **if this biblical book were written
today, how would the author have written it?**

## The balance rule — the operative core

The NLT is explicit that it is *not* purely dynamic, and states the switching
condition precisely:

- Translate **as simply and literally as possible** *when that approach yields an
  accurate, clear, and natural text*. Preserve literary and rhetorical devices,
  ancient metaphors, and word choices that echo across passages.
- Translate **dynamically** when the literal rendering is **hard to understand,
  misleading, or yields archaic or foreign wording**.

> "A purely formal-equivalence translation would be unintelligible…and a purely
> dynamic-equivalence translation would risk being unfaithful to the original."

So: literal is the default; the three failure conditions above are the *only* triggers
for going dynamic. This is a much more disciplined rule than "thought-for-thought"
suggests, and it should be applied as a test, not as a licence.

## Operative rules

| Area | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Weights & measures** | Convert to modern equivalents (ephah, cubit). Footnote the literal Hebrew/Aramaic/Greek. |
| **Currency** | Express in communicative terms: "ten shekels of silver" → "ten pieces of silver"; *denarius* → "the normal daily wage", footnoted. |
| **Dates & months** | Hebrew months → indication of the time of year; where a date is fixable, use the modern calendar (Ezra 6:15 → "March 12", footnoted with the Hebrew and the year 515 BC). |
| **Time of day** | Use the "o'clock" system, or general renderings — "at dawn the next morning", "as the sun began to set". |
| **Proper names** | Where the meaning or a wordplay matters, footnote it — or put it inline in parentheses: "Ishmael (which means 'God hears')". |
| **Cultural gesture** | Translate the sense: "they beat their breasts" → "They went home in deep sorrow", footnoting the literal Greek. Alternative permitted: *illuminate* the literal phrase inline ("beating their breasts in sorrow"), and then **no footnote is needed**. |
| **Metaphor** | Clarify **only where a typical reader would be confused**. "Your neck is like the tower of David" → "as beautiful as the tower of David". |
| **Poetry** | Render as poetry. Break lines to show relationships; represent Hebrew parallelism in natural poetic form. |
| ***hoi Ioudaioi*** | Contextual: "the people" (footnote "the Jewish people") or "the religious leaders" — not a flat "the Jews". |
| **Gender** | *adelphoi* → "brothers and sisters". Generic masculine singular → plural (Prov 22:6 "Direct your children… they") or 2nd person (Prov 26:27 "If you set a trap for others…"). **All masculine language for God is retained without exception.** |
| **Theological jargon** | **Avoid** Latin carry-overs: *justification* → "we are made right with God"; *sanctification* → "we are made holy"; *regeneration* → "we are born anew". |
| **Lexical consistency** | Consistent for divine names, synoptic parallels, repeated rhetorical phrases, and non-theological technical vocabulary (liturgical, legal, cultural, zoological, botanical). Deliberately *wider* semantic range allowed for theological terms. |
| **Proper-name spelling** | One spelling per individual, footnoting divergence (Jehoash of Israel vs. Joash of Judah). "Israel" for the nation, "Jacob" for the man — except where the writer's variant name is theologically purposeful (Eshbaal/Ishbosheth), which is kept with a footnote. |
| **Divine names** | YHWH → "the LORD" (small caps); *’adonai* → "Lord"; *’adonai YHWH* → "Sovereign LORD"; *YHWH ’elohim* → "LORD God"; *YHWH tseba’oth* → **"LORD of Heaven's Armies"** (the name's *meaning*, not a transliteration); "Yahweh" only where the personal character of the name is being contrasted (Ex 3:15; 6:2–3). |
| ***christos*** | "**Messiah**" where a Jewish audience is assumed; "Christ" where a Gentile audience is. (Directly opposite to the ESV's flat consistency.) |
| **Read aloud** | A first-class design constraint: most people will *hear* the text, so it must "make an immediate and powerful impact on any listener." |
| **Vocabulary** | Only language in common use. Avoid what will date quickly or belongs to a narrow sub-dialect. |

## Textual basis

BHS (1977); UBS4 and NA27 (1993). Will follow variant readings from other ancient
witnesses where the evidence supports it, always noted.

## Process

Three specialist scholars per book review a base translation → Senior Translator
consolidates into a first-draft revision → several rounds of exegetical and stylistic
committee review → English stylists work *alongside* scholars throughout, with
scholars reviewing the stylists' edits so that exegetical errors are not introduced
late → the full Bible Translation Committee approves **every verse**. ~90 scholars.

## Notes for the Danish version

- **Use metric.** The NLT converts to American units; the equivalent decision for a
  Danish reader is metric, not a translation of the American figures.
- **Kroner-free, but concrete**: *denarius* → "en almindelig dagløn".
- The anti-Latinate rule is where this version separates hardest from the ESV-style
  one: *retfærdiggørelse* → "gjort rette med Gud"; *helliggørelse* → "gjort hellig";
  *genfødelse* → "født på ny". Danish has strong native alternatives here — use them.
- Danish is well suited to the read-aloud constraint; test renderings by ear.
- YHWH → **HERREN**; *YHWH tseba’oth* → translate the meaning, e.g. "Himlens hære"
  rather than "Sebaot".
- *adelphoi* → "brødre og søstre". Danish *man* and the 2nd person are both natural
  routes out of the generic masculine singular.
- Watch register: contemporary and clear, but the NLT is *not* slang. Avoid anything
  that will read as dated Danish in a decade.
